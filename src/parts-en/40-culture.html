Culture

How a Tamil family lives: what it values, how it eats, dresses and keeps its ties. Follow these and you will fit naturally into Tamil society.

Core values

  • Aram (right conduct): doing what is right; a measure above law and creed. "Desire to do good" is the first line a child learns.
  • Hospitality: a visitor is welcomed with "vaanga" (come in), given water and asked "have you eaten?". To send a guest away unfed is a disgrace.
  • Learning: the Kural calls learning the wealth that cannot be destroyed. A family will sacrifice anything for the children's schooling. Teachers are held high: mother, father, teacher, God.
  • Maanam (honour, self-respect): the root of the modern political word suyamariyathai, self-respect.
  • Giving and sharing: share what you have; help the village.
  • Gratitude: never forget a kindness done to you.
  • Love of the language: Tamil is seen as a mother (Tamil Thai). State functions open with the invocation to Mother Tamil.
  • Equality: "All beings are equal by birth" (Kural) and "Every town is our town" (Sangam) are the ideals Tamils quote today.

Family and kinship

  • The family is the basic unit of society. Caring for parents in old age is a duty.
  • Kin terms are precise: father's elder brother is periyappa, younger is chithappa; mother's elder sister is periyamma, younger is chithi; mother's brother is maama; father's sister is athai. Annan, thambi, akka, thangai mark elder and younger siblings.
  • The mother's brother has a special place. His gifts lead the ear-piercing, coming-of-age and wedding ceremonies.
  • Elders are addressed with the respectful "neengal" and by kin term, not by name. Even strangers can be called anna, akka, amma or aiya.
  • Names: most Tamils use no family name; the father's initial comes first. Using caste names as surnames was largely given up in Tamil Nadu as a result of the Self-Respect Movement.

Life-cycle rites

StageRite
PregnancyValaikaappu: in the seventh or ninth month the mother is adorned with bangles and blessed.
BirthNaming, the cradle ceremony, lullabies.
ChildhoodFirst tonsure; ear-piercing on the uncle's lap; the start of learning, writing the letter அ in rice.
Coming of ageA girl's puberty ceremony (manjal neerattu). In cities it is becoming simpler or is skipped.
MarriageBetrothal, tying the thaali (wedding thread), exchange of garlands, a feast. The self-respect marriage, a vow before the gathering with no religious rite, has been legal since 1967. Christians marry in church and Muslims by nikah; the thaali, silk and feast are common to all.
Old ageThe sixtieth-birthday wedding (manivizha) and the eightieth-year celebration.
DeathLaments (oppari), the parai drum, the funeral procession; cremation or burial; the sixteenth-day rite; yearly remembrance and ancestor worship on the new-moon days of Thai and Aadi.

Food

  • Staple: rice. Earlier, millets such as pearl millet, finger millet, sorghum and foxtail millet fed most people; they are returning.
  • The banana-leaf meal: the tip of the leaf to the diner's left. Salt, pickle, vegetables, kootu, aviyal, appalam; rice in the middle. Courses: dal with ghee, sambar, a tangy kuzhambu, rasam, payasam, and last curd or buttermilk. Eaten with the right hand.
  • Tiffin: idli, dosai, pongal, vadai, upma, idiyappam, aappam, puttu, paniyaram; with chutney, sambar and chilli powder.
  • Flavours: tamarind, pepper, mustard, curry leaf, sesame oil, coconut. The six tastes: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, astringent.
  • Regional food: spiced Chettinad meat dishes; Kongu rice-and-dal and horse gram; Madurai kari dosai and jigarthanda; Nellai halwa and sodhi; Ambur and Dindigul biryani; fish kuzhambu on the coast; Jaffna kool and odiyal; roti canai in Malaysia.
  • Drinks: filter coffee in a tumbler and davara, buttermilk, palm nectar, tender coconut, millet gruel.
  • Most Tamils eat meat; many keep vegetarian days such as Tuesday and Friday.

Dress and adornment

  • Men: veshti and shirt, a towel on the shoulder. The white veshti and shirt is still the mark of the political and ceremonial stage. At home, the lungi.
  • Women: the saree; skirt and blouse for girls; the half-saree (dhavani) for young women; the churidar is now common. Kanchipuram silk for festivals.
  • Ornaments: thaali, toe rings (married women), anklets, nose stud, earrings, bangles; jasmine in the hair. A dot on the forehead; sacred ash for Saivas, the namam for Vaishnavas.
  • Weaving centres: Kanchipuram, Arani, Thirubuvanam (silk); Madurai sungudi; Chettinad kandangi; Coimbatore and Chinnalapatti (cotton); Bhavani carpets.

Home and daily order

  • Kolam: at dawn the threshold is sprinkled with water and a pattern is drawn around dots with rice flour, food for the ants too. Larger kolams in Margazhi, with a pumpkin flower at the centre.
  • Thinnai: the raised verandah at the front of a traditional house where passers-by may rest. Courtyard houses; the mansions of Chettinad.
  • No footwear indoors. Feet must not touch books, food or elders. Give and receive with the right hand.
  • A lamp is lit at dusk; on Fridays the house is cleaned for worship.
  • Many choose an auspicious day for new ventures; those of the rationalist tradition do not. Both are part of Tamil society today.

Reckoning time

Months: Chithirai, Vaikasi, Aani, Aadi, Aavani, Purattasi, Aippasi, Karthigai, Margazhi, Thai, Maasi, Panguni. The year begins on the first of Chithirai (about 14 April). The Thiruvalluvar era (counted from 31 BCE) appears on Tamil Nadu government documents.

Six seasons: early summer (Chithirai, Vaikasi), high summer (Aani, Aadi), rains (Aavani, Purattasi), cold season (Aippasi, Karthigai), early dew (Margazhi, Thai), late dew (Maasi, Panguni).

Days: Nyayiru (Sunday), Thingal, Sevvai, Budhan, Viyazhan, Velli, Sani.

Caste and social justice

To understand Tamil society honestly, this too must be known. A hierarchy of caste by birth, and untouchability, prevailed for centuries; their effects are still felt in marriage, village layout and politics. At the same time a voice against caste has sounded strongly from within the Tamil tradition itself: Valluvar, the Siddhars, Vallalar, Iyothee Thass, Periyar. Reservation in education and jobs, and the right of people of all castes to become temple priests, are fruits of that struggle. A community learning from this treasury should take up the tradition of equality, not the hierarchy.

Tamil life today

  • Tamil Nadu is among India's most urbanised and industrialised states, and a leader in literacy, girls' education, college enrolment and public health.
  • Most young people know Tamil and English. Engineering, medicine and information technology are favoured fields.
  • Cinema, cricket, televised debates and political talk at the tea shop are everyday pastimes.
  • City dwellers still return to their native village for the temple festival, the family deity and Pongal.
  • Love marriages and inter-caste marriages are increasing; arranged marriage continues too.
